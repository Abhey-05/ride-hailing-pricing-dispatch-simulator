# Experiments — Results & Analysis

This document reports what the 1,080-run experiment matrix (`docs/EXPERIMENT_DESIGN.md`) actually found. Every number here is read directly from `results/results.csv`, `results/core_comparisons.csv`, and `results/decision_table.csv`, produced by `python run_experiments.py && python analyze_results.py` — nothing is hand-typed or adjusted after the fact. Where a result surprised us, it's reported as found, not smoothed over (see Experiment 6 and the Advanced Heuristic finding).

**Reproduce this document's numbers:** `python run_experiments.py && python analyze_results.py`, then read `results/*.csv`.

---

## Experiment 1 — Effect of surge multiplier on rider demand

**Hypothesis:** higher realized surge suppresses rider demand (lower acceptance of priced offers).
**Setup:** compare the 4 pricing policies (core matrix, `NORMAL` scenario, 24 seeds, dispatch held at all 5 policies and averaged).
**Independent variable:** pricing policy (→ realized price index). **Dependent variable:** rider conversion rate.

| Pricing policy | Price index (realized/no-surge) | Rider conversion rate |
|---|---|---|
| NO_SURGE | 1.000 | 55.06% |
| BASIC_SURGE | 1.007 | 55.04% |
| CAPPED_SMOOTHED_SURGE | 1.017 | 55.14% |
| AGGRESSIVE_SURGE | 1.065 | 54.73% |

**Result:** the demand-suppression effect is real but small in this model at the realized surge levels the marketplace actually reaches (surge rarely sustains near its cap — see Experiment 6) — a ~6.5% price increase (AGGRESSIVE_SURGE) corresponds to only a ~0.3–0.4 percentage-point drop in conversion.
**Interpretation:** the rider price-sensitivity function (Math Model §C) is doing what it's designed to do, but the *marketplace-level* effect is muted because AGGRESSIVE_SURGE also improves wait time enough to keep conversion from collapsing (see Experiment 3).
**Product implication:** in this model, moderate surge does not visibly damage top-line conversion — but this is a function of the specific elasticity parameters chosen (synthetic), not a general claim; see the sensitivity analysis caveat in Limitations.
**Limitation:** price elasticity (`β_price`) is a synthetic assumption, not fit to real rider behavior — a more elastic population would show a larger effect here.

## Experiment 2 — Effect of surge pricing on driver supply

**Hypothesis:** surge pulls in more driver supply.
**Result:** **it does not, in this model** — validated explicitly as engine invariant (`tests/test_engine_invariants.py::test_pricing_does_not_change_fleet_supply`): driver online-hours are generated by the exogenous shift-schedule model (Math Model §E.1) and are, by construction, identical across all four pricing policies at a given seed.
**Interpretation:** this is a **modeling limitation, not a discovery** — v1 does not implement a supply-response-to-price mechanism (e.g., drivers deciding to log on because surge is active nearby). Real platforms do see this effect; it is the single most consequential missing mechanism in this simulation and is flagged prominently in `docs/CRITICAL_REVIEW.md`.
**Product implication:** any conclusion in this report about surge's ability to *relieve a supply shortage* should be read as "surge helps by rationing demand and reducing pickup congestion," not "surge helps by attracting more drivers" — the latter is real in production but not modeled here.
**What would fix it:** a driver online-probability function conditioned on current/recent local surge, with its own patience/reservation-wage parameters — flagged as the top item in future work.

## Experiment 3 — Effect of surge on platform revenue

**Setup:** same as Experiment 1.

| Pricing policy | Platform revenue (currency/day) | Cancellation rate |
|---|---|---|
| NO_SURGE | 89,989 | 21.68% |
| BASIC_SURGE | 90,652 | 20.65% |
| CAPPED_SMOOTHED_SURGE | 91,653 | 20.24% |
| AGGRESSIVE_SURGE | 95,366 | 16.48% |

**Result:** revenue rises monotonically with surge aggressiveness (+6.0% for AGGRESSIVE_SURGE vs. NO_SURGE) — and, notably, **cancellation rate falls at the same time** (21.7% → 16.5%).
**Interpretation:** this is the clearest illustration of surge's real economic logic in the simulation: higher realized prices bring in enough marginal supply-side relief (shorter waits, since fewer riders are in the queue and imbalance eases) that cancellations from *impatience* drop faster than acceptance drops from *price* — a net positive for both revenue and (for the riders who do get a ride) experience. This is a textbook market-clearing effect, and it emerged from the simulation rather than being assumed.
**Product implication:** in this model, the case against surge is not "it kills revenue" (it doesn't) — it's the guardrail risk (rider price index, brand/regulatory concerns) that isn't captured by a same-day revenue number. See Experiment 6 and the Part 14 decision matrix in `docs/PRD.md`.
**Limitation:** no churn modeling — a real platform's multi-week revenue could look very different if riders who experience repeated surge stop opening the app at all, an effect this single-day model cannot see.

## Experiment 4 — Effect of dispatch strategy on wait time (the primary hypothesis)

**Hypothesis (pre-registered):** a better dispatch policy reduces P90 rider wait time relative to `NEAREST_DRIVER`, without an offsetting rise in driver-side cost.
**Setup:** paired comparison across 24 common-random-number seeds, `NORMAL` scenario, for every (pricing, dispatch) pair vs. the same pricing paired with `NEAREST_DRIVER`.

| Dispatch policy | P90 wait vs. baseline (avg across 4 pricing policies) | Statistically + practically significant? |
|---|---|---|
| **ETA_OPTIMIZED** | **−9.1%** (range −7.8% to −11.3%) | **Yes, in all 4 pricing policies** |
| DRIVER_EARNINGS_AWARE | +0.4% to +2.4% (small improvement) | No — CI includes 0 in 3/4 pricing policies |
| MARKETPLACE_AWARE | −0.8% to +1.2% | No — CI includes 0 in all 4 |
| ADVANCED_HEURISTIC | **−4.5% to −8.3% (worse)** | No — statistically significant, but in the *wrong* direction |

**Result:** `ETA_OPTIMIZED` is the only policy that clears the pre-registered bar (≥5% relative improvement AND 95% CI excluding zero), and it does so in **every** pricing policy tested, with very small p-values (all < 0.001, several < 10⁻⁴) and a large effect size (matched-pairs Cohen's d around −1.5 to −2.0 across pricing policies — a very large effect by conventional standards).
**Interpretation:** the mechanism is exactly what Math Model §H.2 predicted: `ETA_OPTIMIZED`'s look-ahead to soon-to-be-free drivers gives it access to a genuinely larger, better candidate pool than any policy restricted to "currently idle" drivers, including `NEAREST_DRIVER` itself.
**Product implication:** this is the project's central, load-bearing finding — see the Recommendation section of `docs/PRD.md`.
**Limitation:** all four dispatch policies operate under the same synthetic driver-acceptance model; a real fleet's actual accept/reject behavior could interact with a look-ahead policy differently (e.g., drivers dislike being told about a pickup before they've dropped off their current rider) — untested here.

## Experiment 5 — Effect of dispatch strategy on driver utilization

| Dispatch policy | Driver utilization vs. baseline | Earnings/online-hour vs. baseline |
|---|---|---|
| ETA_OPTIMIZED | **−1.5%** | **+1.9%** |
| DRIVER_EARNINGS_AWARE | +2 to +3% | +0.3 to +7.6% (AGGRESSIVE_SURGE) |
| ADVANCED_HEURISTIC | −2 to −4% | −5% to −7% |

**Result (the interesting, non-obvious one):** `ETA_OPTIMIZED` **completes more trips per driver while its utilization metric (active-time share of online time) goes slightly *down*.**
**Interpretation:** utilization is defined as `(pickup + trip time) / online time` (Math Model §J). `ETA_OPTIMIZED` succeeds specifically by cutting *wasted pickup driving* — shorter pickup legs mean less "committed but not yet carrying a rider" time counted as active, even as more trips get completed and earnings/hour rises. This is a genuine reason utilization and "trips per hour" are *not* interchangeable metrics, and it's exactly why the North Star is defined as *trips* per online-hour, not *utilization* — a policy that looks slightly worse on one diagnostic can still be strictly better on the metric that matters.
**Product implication:** don't ship "raise utilization" as a target metric on its own — it can be gamed or simply misleading, as shown here.

## Experiment 6 — Pricing × dispatch interaction

**Setup:** the full 4×5 decision table (`results/decision_table.csv`).
**Result:** the two levers are **additive, not interactive** in this model — `ETA_OPTIMIZED` improves P90 wait by a similar relative amount under every pricing policy (7.8%–11.3%), and the *best combination found* (`CAPPED_SMOOTHED_SURGE` + `ETA_OPTIMIZED`, North Star 1.994) is barely different from the *second-best* (`BASIC_SURGE` + `ETA_OPTIMIZED`, North Star 1.990) — a 0.2% difference, well within noise.
**Interpretation:** there is no strong evidence in this model that pricing policy choice matters much once the better dispatch policy is in place — the dispatch effect dominates. This is itself informative: it suggests the two investments (pricing sophistication vs. dispatch sophistication) can be prioritized independently rather than needing to be co-designed.
**Product implication:** ship the dispatch improvement first; pricing policy choice among the three surge variants tested is a much lower-stakes decision by comparison.
**Limitation:** this conclusion is specific to the 4 pricing policies actually implemented — a fundamentally different pricing mechanism (e.g., per-rider personalized pricing) was out of scope and could interact differently.

## Experiment 7 — Demand shock (robustness)

**Setup:** `DEMAND_SHOCK` scenario (a +150% demand spike in one random zone for 90 minutes), 6 seeds, `BASIC_SURGE` pricing.
**Result:** `ETA_OPTIMIZED` P90 wait: 9.57 min vs. 10.50 min baseline (**−8.9%**), Wilcoxon p = 0.0625 (not significant at α=0.05 with only 6 seeds — see note below).
**Interpretation:** the effect size is consistent with the core result, but 6 seeds gives materially less statistical power than the core experiment's 24 — this is a case where the point estimate is encouraging but the evidence is not yet conclusive on its own.
**Product implication:** the dispatch improvement appears to survive a localized demand shock, but this specific scenario would benefit from more seeds before being cited as a standalone result.

## Experiment 8 — Supply shortage (robustness)

**Setup:** `SUPPLY_SHORTAGE` scenario (60% of normal fleet), 6 seeds.
**Result:** P90 wait 12.83 min vs. 13.83 min baseline (**−7.2%**), Wilcoxon p = 0.031 (significant).
**Interpretation:** dispatch quality still helps materially even when the underlying problem is a genuine shortage of drivers, not a matching inefficiency — better matching cannot manufacture supply, but it measurably reduces how badly a shortage is felt.
**Product implication:** dispatch improvement is not a substitute for fixing a real structural supply shortage (see Experiment 2's limitation — this model can't test driver-supply response to incentives), but it meaningfully softens the impact while a supply fix is pursued.

## Experiment 9 — Peak-hour scenario (robustness)

**Setup:** `PEAK_DEMAND` scenario (1.6× baseline demand), 6 seeds.
**Result:** P90 wait 11.83 min vs. 13.00 min baseline (**−9.0%**), Wilcoxon p = 0.031 (significant) — the largest absolute-minute improvement (1.17 min) of any robustness scenario.
**Interpretation:** the dispatch improvement's value *increases* somewhat under peak load, consistent with the mechanism (more soon-to-be-free drivers exist to look ahead to when the marketplace is busier).
**Product implication:** if forced to prioritize where to enable the new dispatch policy first, peak hours are where it earns its keep fastest.

## Experiment 10 — Low-demand scenario (robustness)

**Setup:** `LOW_DEMAND` scenario (0.6× baseline demand), 6 seeds.
**Result:** P90 wait 7.67 min vs. 8.17 min baseline (**−6.1%**), Wilcoxon p = 0.25 (**not significant**).
**Interpretation:** this is the expected, honest null-ish result — when the marketplace is already well-supplied relative to demand, there is less room for a smarter dispatch policy to matter, and the smaller absolute wait times leave less to improve on the primary metric.
**Product implication:** don't expect the dispatch investment to show a dramatic result in an already-slack market — it's a peak/shortage/shock tool more than a quiet-Tuesday-afternoon tool.

## Experiment 11 — Policy robustness across random seeds

**Setup:** the core experiment's 24-seed design *is* this check — every headline number in Experiment 4 already carries its cross-seed variance in its reported confidence interval.
**Result:** `ETA_OPTIMIZED`'s improvement is consistent in sign and magnitude across all 24 seeds under `BASIC_SURGE` (bootstrap 95% CI: 6.9%–11.0% relative improvement, i.e., the CI never crosses into "no effect" or "harmful"), and across all 4 pricing policies.
**Interpretation:** this is the strongest single piece of evidence in the project that the headline finding is not a lucky simulation run.

## Experiment 12 — Sensitivity analysis

**Setup:** `docs/EXPERIMENT_DESIGN.md` §W's ±30% perturbation sweep, run via `sensitivity_analysis.py`, on the winning comparison (`ETA_OPTIMIZED` vs. `NEAREST_DRIVER` P90 wait, `BASIC_SURGE`/`NORMAL`, 8 seeds per condition). Each of the six most uncertain parameters was perturbed ±30% independently, holding everything else at its base value.

| Parameter perturbed | ×0.7 | ×1.3 | Sign preserved both directions? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baseline (no perturbation) | — | −9.45% | — |
| Price elasticity (β_price) | −8.68% | −6.25% | Yes |
| Rider patience | −10.30% | −7.97% | Yes |
| Driver acceptance sensitivity (γ_pickup/γ_fare) | −6.25% | −10.95% | Yes |
| Congestion multiplier | −9.20% | −13.30% | Yes |
| Demand intensity (λ_base) | −5.59% | −7.67% | Yes |
| Supply intensity (fleet size) | −4.20% | −7.53% | Yes |

**Result:** the sign of the core finding (`ETA_OPTIMIZED` improves P90 wait) survives every single one of the 12 perturbations — it never flips to zero or reverses. The magnitude does move (from −4.2% at its smallest to −13.3% at its largest), most sensitively to supply intensity (a 30% smaller fleet nearly halves the effect, from −9.45% to −4.20%) and congestion (a 30% congestion increase nearly increases it to −13.3%).
**Interpretation:** the *qualitative* conclusion — dispatch quality matters and helps — is robust to this project's biggest single-parameter uncertainties. The *exact* 8.9% headline number is not: a real deployment should expect somewhere in the roughly 4–13% range depending on the true values of these synthetic parameters, not treat 8.9% as a precise prediction.
**Product implication:** this is enough evidence to justify a real shadow-mode test (the conclusion isn't fragile to any one wrong guess), but not enough to promise a specific percentage improvement to a business stakeholder — the recommendation in `docs/PRD.md` §14 is phrased accordingly ("7.8–11.3% across pricing policies," a range, not a point).
**Limitation:** perturbations were tested one parameter at a time, not in combination — a real worst-case (e.g., low elasticity *and* low fleet size *and* low congestion simultaneously) was not tested and could plausibly push the effect closer to (though the individual-parameter results suggest not past) zero.

## The Advanced Heuristic finding (reported, not hidden)

`ADVANCED_HEURISTIC` was designed to be the "best" policy — a weighted combination of pickup ETA, marketplace-imbalance protection, destination-steering, and cancellation-risk urgency (Math Model §H.5). It performed **worse than the naive `NEAREST_DRIVER` baseline on P90 wait in every single pricing policy tested** (−4.5% to −8.3%, all statistically significant in the *wrong* direction), and also worse on driver earnings and utilization.

This was not the expected result, and it was not adjusted after the fact. The most likely explanation, based on the weights in `src/config.py` (`ADVANCED_HEURISTIC_WEIGHTS`): the marketplace-imbalance penalty (`w_imb=3.0`) and cancellation-urgency term (`w_cancel=1.5`) are large enough, relative to the pickup-ETA term (`w_eta=1.0`), that the policy frequently accepts a much longer pickup in service of a marginal improvement to a *different* zone's future balance — a real risk with hand-tuned multi-objective heuristics that this project's own documentation (Math Model §H.5) warned about in the abstract before the data confirmed it in practice. **The lesson generalizes: a dispatch policy that "considers more things" is not automatically better — the relative weights need their own validation, which this project did not do for `ADVANCED_HEURISTIC` before including it in the experiment matrix.** This is exactly the kind of finding a real dispatch team would want caught in simulation before it reached production.

## Statistical notes (read before citing any number above)

- **"1,080 runs" ≠ "1,080 independent samples."** The core hypothesis test (Experiment 4) has **24** independent seeds per comparison; common random numbers make those 24 comparisons far more sensitive than 24 unpaired runs would be, but they do not multiply into 480 or 1,080 independent data points for any single question.
- Every "significant" result above is significant by **both** the pre-registered practical bar (≥5% relative effect, CI excludes 0) **and** a formal test (paired Wilcoxon), per `docs/EXPERIMENT_DESIGN.md` §Q — a result meeting only one of these is called out as such (e.g., Experiment 7).
- Mean vs. median: P90 wait differences were checked for mean/median divergence per seed; no run showed a qualitatively different conclusion between the two, so means are reported throughout for readability.
